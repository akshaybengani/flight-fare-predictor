What FareSight does

FareSight shows the fare listed for a flight next to the fare a computer model expects. It also flags flights where the model expects the price to settle lower later.

The prices are estimates from past data. They are not live prices and not advice to book.

Is it real or made up?

The fares are real, but they are old and they are not live. They were copied from public booking sites by other people and shared on Kaggle. They are not an airline's own feed. We did not make up or fill in any fare ourselves.

  • We downloaded a few other sets that looked artificial (one for Bangladesh and one linking oil and fares). We judged them synthetic and did not use them.
  • Because the India data is from 2022 and 2023, it still lists airlines that have stopped flying (GO FIRST, Vistara, AirAsia India). They are hidden by default.
  • Each India flight's price was recorded once per collection day. The model never saw one flight's price change over time.

Does fuel price matter?

A short, honest answer.

  • In the India model: no, it is not used. We tested fuel and exchange-rate columns. They added nothing we could measure. The India data are single-day snapshots, so fuel cannot be told apart from everything else that changed between 2022 and 2023.
  • In the US model: yes, it is included, but its effect is tiny. Taking fuel out changes accuracy by about 0.2 points at most, and we cannot say it is cause and effect.
  • In the real world: it matters. Fuel is roughly a third of an airline's costs. It reaches fares with a delay, as higher base fares or fuel surcharges.

How the model learns

Five steps, in plain words.

Want to see the arithmetic? How the price is calculated lets you run a tiny version yourself, then shows the real model working on a real flight.

  1. Collect. Take hundreds of thousands of real flights, each with its facts and its price.
  2. Hide some. Keep a share of the flights back. The model never sees them while learning.
  3. Learn rules. The model builds many small "if this, then that" questions, like: is the typical price of this flight above ₹8,000? Is it 3 days before departure?
  4. Fix mistakes. Each new set of questions is built to correct the errors the earlier ones still make. This repeats thousands of times.
  5. Test on the hidden flights. Compare its prices with the real ones. That gives the accuracy score.

The accuracy score, step by step

Try it with two numbers. The first box is what a flight really cost. The second is what the model said.

The difference is ₹50.
As a share of the real price that is 2.5% (the percentage miss).
This flight's score is 100 − 2.5 = 97.5.
Within 10%? yes.

The overall score is this flight score averaged over thousands of flights the model has not seen. A few big misses pull the average down.

Why are there three scores? They are three different tests, from easy to hard.

    93% does not mean 93 of 100 flights are right. It means the average miss is about 7%. Only about 78 of 100 flights land within 10% of the real price.

    The algorithms

    Each card is one way of turning the 18 facts into a price. The scores are the same three tests as above (same period / small recent sample / unseen period).

    Why XGBoost is the default, and why HistGradientBoosting came first

    HistGradientBoosting was the first model because it comes ready-made inside scikit-learn, a common Python toolkit. XGBoost is the same family of method. On our tests it is ahead by a few tenths of a point in two of the three, so we switched to it. HistGradientBoosting stays as the back-up: if XGBoost cannot load on a computer, FareSight uses it and says so.

    What is Approve?

    The original idea was a review step. A pricing manager looks at the model's price for a flight and approves it to replace the listed price.

    In this demo, Approve only marks flights on your screen as a shortlist. Nothing is booked, saved on a server or sent anywhere. The shortlist is forgotten when you close the browser tab.

    Questions a client may ask

    Are these live prices?
    No. India uses fares collected in 2022 and 2023. The US set is a snapshot from 30 September 2026. Nothing here is connected to a booking site.
    How accurate is it?
    On flights from a period it has seen, about 93% accurate. On a period it has never seen, about 68%.
    Which cities does it cover?
    Seven Indian cities. The US market has 50 routes between major airports.
    Does it use festivals, weather or fuel?
    The India model does not. We tried them and they did not help. The US model includes holidays, weather averages and fuel, and they add very little.
    Why do some flights show a big jump?
    The listed fare is a fare copied from a booking site near that booking window. The model's price is for one day before departure. A big gap is mostly last-minute pricing in the data, not a forecast.
    Can I trust a "May drop" or "Big save"?
    Treat it as a hint. It means the model expects the price to settle lower. It is an estimate, not a promise.
    What would make it better?
    Prices captured every day for the same flights, so the model can watch a fare move, plus seat availability. Those two are what the current data lacks.
